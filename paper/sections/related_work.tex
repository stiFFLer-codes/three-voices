\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Two literatures frame the design: stakeholder-tailored explanation and
selective prediction. Three more set the standard it has to meet: explainable
AI for community health workers, risk
communication without numeracy, and the published work on this dataset. The
five subsections below take them in that order.

\subsection{Stakeholder-tailored explanation}
\label{sec:rw-stakeholder}

Two of our three tiers reproduce a split this literature settled before us.
Arya et al.\ made the point in a title, \emph{One Explanation Does Not Fit
All}, and built the toolkit and taxonomy to go with
it~\citep{arya2019explanation}. Imrie et al.\ examine the objectives and
requirements of different stakeholders in healthcare and argue that they call
for different types of interpretability~\citep{imrie2023multiple}. Kim et al.\
build and compare stakeholder-tailored explanation interfaces directly,
including that same clinician-versus-patient
split~\citep{kim2024stakeholder}. Bello et al.\ propose a
\emph{three-level} pipeline from technical explanation to natural language,
which is our structural twin~\citep{bello2025threelevel}. The
audience-specific split and tiering as an architecture both predate this work.

Where we diverge from Bello et al.\ is the mediator. Theirs is a large
language model. Ours is a
fixed set of templates, selected by the band and filled from the SHAP
attributions, so every string a health worker
or a mother receives is determined by the case and can be regenerated and
audited. Nothing is generated at inference time and there is nothing to
hallucinate. We give up fluency and coverage for a rendering an auditor can
check line by line.

We name our tiers by role, and Suresh et al.\ argue that role is the wrong
axis: a stakeholder's role determines neither the knowledge they bring nor the
interpretability task they face, and they offer a typology that decouples the
two~\citep{suresh2021beyond}. The objection holds. Those names are shorthand,
and what varies across the tiers is what each reader lacks, in reading
modality, literacy and decision authority; each tier is designed against that
deficit. In this deployment role and deficit track each other closely, since
the ASHA is a specific, minimally-schooled cadre and the mother may not read
at all. Szymanski et al.\ propose treating role and expertise as separate
dimensions, because the field has often conflated
them~\citep{szymanski2025disentangling}. Where role and the reader's deficit
come apart, a deployment would have to select on the deficit.

\subsection{Uncertainty communication, abstention, and selective prediction}
\label{sec:rw-uncertainty}

Our gate declines the highest-severity signal when the top-two class
probabilities are separated by less than a fixed margin. That is a hand-set
instance of the reject option Hendrickx et al.\ survey, and a cruder one:
their taxonomy names the case we implement, rejection under \emph{ambiguity},
where two classes are close, as distinct from rejection under
novelty~\citep{hendrickx2024reject}. Mozannar and Sontag give the
learning-to-defer version, where the model is trained to route a case to a
human expert rather than answer it~\citep{mozannar2020consistent}. From Kompa
et al.\ we take the clinical framing that licenses derating a signal instead
of issuing it: in medical machine learning, abstaining on high-uncertainty
cases is a safety property~\citep{kompa2021second}.

What we add sits one step downstream, and Bhatt et al.\ are the closest prior
work to it~\citep{bhatt2021uncertainty}. They treat uncertainty as itself a
form of transparency and consider explicitly how it should be
\emph{communicated to different stakeholders}. The difference is narrow: they
make the \emph{display} of uncertainty audience-aware, whereas we make the
\emph{suppression} of an assertion audience-aware. The mother receives a
different assertion, while the clinician receives the split probabilities
unmodified. Section~\ref{sec:tiers} gives the rule that grades those
assertions. We find no prior work that makes the abstention \emph{rendering} a
function of what each stakeholder can interrogate.

\subsection{Explainable AI for community health workers in the Global South}
\label{sec:rw-chw}

Our second tier renders to a community health worker, and this is the
literature that sets the standard it has to meet. Okolo et al.'s systematic
review of explainable AI in the Global South found 16 papers, of which 3
involved people and 1 attempted deployment with target
users~\citep{okolo2022makingai}.

Studies with community health workers in India set our context. Okolo et al.\
interviewed community health
workers in rural India about AI-enabled mobile health tools and about
explanations specifically~\citep{okolo2021cannot,okolo2024easy}.
Solano-Kamaiko et al.\ built and studied an explorable explanation interface
with the same cadre~\citep{solanokamaiko2024explorable}. Srinidhi et al.\
document ASHA Kirana, a deployment in which ASHAs collect data that feeds an
algorithm and returns a report for a doctor~\citep{srinidhi2021asha}, which is
our data flow, minus the tiering and the uncertainty gate.

Two findings in that context bound what our card can be said to do. Okolo
et al.\ found that community health workers struggled to understand the AI
explanations they were shown, while still wanting explanations in their
tools~\citep{okolo2024easy}. Solano-Kamaiko et al.\ found that explorable
explanations, which pair visual heuristics with written text and invite active
exploration, helped health workers build a more nuanced understanding of AI
and contest its decisions~\citep{solanokamaiko2024explorable}. Comprehension
therefore depends on design and has to be tested. Our card has not been
tested, which is why a user study is the next step. We propose a rendering
rule and evaluate the artifact that implements it.

\subsection{Communicating risk without numeracy}
\label{sec:rw-riskcomm}

Our third tier renders to a reader who may not read, and this literature both
warrants that tier and argues against how we built it. Galesic et al.\ show
that icon arrays let low-numeracy readers extract risk information they
otherwise miss~\citep{galesic2009icon}, and Garc\'{i}a-Retamero and Cokely's
systematic review finds visual aids improve risk literacy most among the
vulnerable readers our tier targets~\citep{garciaretamero2017visual}. Richter
et al.\ add the negative result that matters most for a voice channel: purely
verbal communication of benefits and harms performs poorly for patients with
limited health literacy~\citep{richter2023communication}, which is why our
spoken message is paired with a visual rather than shipped alone. Peters et
al.\ collect current best practice for numeric risk~\citep{peters2025numeric},
and Bradley et al.\ test risk presentation in the maternity
setting~\citep{bradley2025birth}.

That literature recommends conveying \emph{magnitude} transparently, through
an icon array or a visible denominator. Our mother tier strips magnitude
entirely and renders an ordinal band plus a single action, which read flatly
is what the field advises against. We take the departure on a condition about
when a magnitude is the sender's to assert, and state it in
Section~\ref{sec:tiers} beside the tier it governs.

\subsection{Machine learning on the UCI Maternal Health Risk dataset}
\label{sec:rw-dataset}

We build on the ensemble and SHAP pipeline this dataset has standardised,
which puts what is ours downstream of it, in the rendering. Supervised classification with post-hoc attribution here is
saturated: Mamun et al.~\citep{mamun2025identification}, Maisoon et
al.~\citep{maisoon2026stratification}, Widyawati et
al.~\citep{widyawati2025ensemble}, Rahman et al.~\citep{rahman2023xai},
Maheswari et al.~\citep{maheswari2024smote} and Hossen et
al.~\citep{hossen2024driven} all fit ensemble classifiers to this dataset and
explain them post hoc, most with SHAP, and three resample for class imbalance
with SMOTE. We use the same pipeline as a vehicle.

Maisoon et al.\ are our closest methodological neighbour, so we separate the
two explicitly~\citep{maisoon2026stratification}. They use the same dataset with
a two-row outlier removal, tree ensembles with SMOTE, stratified
five-fold cross-validation, SHAP, and the same low-resource framing.
Everything upstream of the explanation is substantially their pipeline. What
is ours lies downstream: deduplicating before splitting and reporting what
that costs (Section~\ref{sec:data}), gating the rendering on the model's own
uncertainty, and rendering the gated output three ways. Widyawati
et al.\ rank blood sugar and systolic blood pressure at the top, as ours do,
and Maisoon et al.\ name blood sugar and blood pressure among the primary
determinants~\citep{widyawati2025ensemble,maisoon2026stratification}, which
corroborates the attributions rather than adding a finding of ours.

Yesmin et al.\ are the closest contemporaneous
neighbour~\citep{yesmin2026trust}.
% Source (yesmin2026trust, arXiv abstract): "A validation study with 14
% healthcare professionals in Bangladesh revealed strong preference for hybrid
% explanations (71.4% across three clinical cases)"
They pair ante-hoc fuzzy rules with post-hoc SHAP on this dataset and
validate the result with 14 healthcare professionals in Bangladesh, who showed
a strong preference for the hybrid explanation (71.4\% across three clinical
cases). Their question is which explanation format healthcare professionals
trust; ours is whether an assertion should be issued at all, and nothing in
their pipeline withholds one when the model is uncertain. Their validation
also sits on the one tier our gate leaves untouched. Their study does not ask
what a health worker or a mother makes of a derated signal.

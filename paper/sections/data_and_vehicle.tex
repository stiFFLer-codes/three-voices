\section{Data and the Model Vehicle}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:data-dataset}

We use the UCI Maternal Health Risk dataset~\citep{ahmed2020maternal}
(repository id 863), released under CC BY 4.0. The distributed file contains
1014 records collected in rural Bangladesh, while the repository page lists
1013 instances~\citep{ahmed2020maternal}.
% 1013: read off the UCI repository page and API for id 863 on 2026-10-09
% ("Instances": 1013; num_instances 1013). External fact, cited per
% WRITING_RULES §5; also recorded in STATE.md Decision Log #60.
Each record has six features (age, systolic and
diastolic blood pressure, blood sugar, body temperature and heart rate) and a
three-level risk label: low, mid, high. It is public data and no other data
is used anywhere in this work. No real patient record, de-identified or otherwise, appears in this
paper or in the repository that accompanies it.

The dataset supports \emph{generic maternal-health risk} and nothing
narrower. It carries no label for any specific condition, and none of the
laboratory, urinary or obstetric-history fields that would let one be
inferred. We use the generic term throughout, in the manuscript, in the code
and in every rendered artifact, and no tier names a condition.

The label is a risk stratification recorded alongside the readings, not a
delivered outcome. Nothing in this paper should be read as a prediction of
what happens to any patient, and that constraint binds the third tier
hardest: a spoken message naming a diagnosis would assert something the data
cannot support. What we demonstrate is an explanation design over a model
fit to this public dataset.

\subsection{Deduplication, and why it happens before the split}
\label{sec:data-dedup}

Two preprocessing steps are applied, both before any train/test split, and
both chosen so that neither learns a statistic from the data.

First, two records report a heart rate of 7 beats per
minute~\citep{ahmed2020maternal}. These are
physiologically impossible and are dropped, leaving 1012 rows. Second, the
remaining set contains a large number of exactly repeated feature-and-label
vectors: 561 of the 1012 rows are exact duplicates of an earlier row. We
drop them, leaving 451 distinct records: 233 low, 106 mid and 112
high, an imbalance of about 2.2:1 between the largest and smallest class.
% 7 bpm: an external fact about the dataset, cited. Two rows in the cached raw
% file (data/raw/maternal_health_risk.csv, 1014 rows) have HeartRate 7.0, and
% the next lowest value is 60.0. The code drops HeartRate < 20:
% src/model.py:65 (MIN_PLAUSIBLE_HR = 20) and :96-97. The count of 2 is
% p1_cohort_summary.csv dropped_impossible_heartrate.
% 2.2:1 = 233 / 106 = 2.198: p1_cohort_summary.csv,
% class_distribution_clean.low risk and class_distribution_clean.mid risk.

Both steps are fixed rules rather than fitted transformations, which is why
performing them before the split introduces no leakage: nothing about the
test fold informs what is dropped. Everything that \emph{does} learn from
data, meaning feature scaling and SMOTE where a configuration used it, is
fitted inside each cross-validation training fold only, through an
\texttt{imblearn} pipeline. We considered the alternative, which is to retain
the duplicates under group-aware cross-validation and keep more rows. We
rejected it on two grounds: it adds machinery a vehicle model does not need,
and deduplication makes our number directly comparable to what a reader gets
from \texttt{drop\_duplicates} applied after the heart-rate filter, the order
we use.

Deduplication does not resolve every ambiguity in the data. Among the 451
distinct records there remain 35 feature vectors that carry more than
one risk label: identical six-value readings recorded against different
levels of risk. No model, however specified, can separate rows it cannot
distinguish. We retain these rows rather than adjudicating between them, and
return to what they imply for the achievable ceiling in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{A leakage-aware re-benchmark: what the duplicates were worth}
\label{sec:data-leakage}

The duplicate rows inflate the scores. A duplicated row can appear in
a training fold and in the test fold of the same split, so retaining them
lets a model be scored partly on records it has already memorised. We
measured that effect directly. Scoring the identical model under the
identical five-fold cross-validation twice, changing only whether duplicates
were dropped, gives Table~\ref{tab:leakage}
(\texttt{results/tables/p1\_duplicate\_leakage.csv}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{The same model and the same cross-validation, scored with exact
duplicate rows dropped and retained. Mean $\pm$ standard deviation across
five stratified folds.}
\label{tab:leakage}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Duplicates & $n$ & Macro-recall & Accuracy & High-risk recall \\
\midrule
Dropped & 451  & $0.580 \pm 0.027$ & $0.641 \pm 0.025$ & $0.723 \pm 0.054$ \\
Kept    & 1012 & $0.859 \pm 0.026$ & $0.854 \pm 0.026$ & $0.908 \pm 0.031$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Retaining the duplicates moves accuracy from 0.641 to 0.854 and macro-recall
from 0.580 to 0.859. The inflated \emph{accuracy}, 0.854, is the like-for-like
comparator for the published figures, which are accuracies, and it sits inside
the range reported by the papers below; the published range extends
higher still. Mamun et al.\ report 99.51\% accuracy on all 1014 records under
10-fold cross-validation~\citep{mamun2025identification}. Maisoon et al.\
remove two implausible outliers ($N = 1012$) and report 83.74\% accuracy at a
tuned decision threshold of 0.67~\citep{maisoon2026stratification}. Neither removes duplicate
rows~\citep{mamun2025identification,maisoon2026stratification}. Yesmin et
al.\ report 88.67\% on all 1014 records and likewise describe no duplicate handling, though that figure is not a
like-for-like comparison: they add a synthetic regional access score to the
six clinical features~\citep{yesmin2026trust}.

Leakage is well
documented: Kapoor and Narayanan set out its taxonomy and its role in the
reproducibility crisis~\citep{kapoor2023leakage}, Rosenblatt et al.\ show
that leakage through repeated subjects inflates performance and that the
effect is worst on small datasets, which is our
situation~\citep{rosenblatt2024leakage}, and Bernett et al.\ give the
practitioner's checklist~\citep{bernett2024guiding}. Measuring what leakage
costs has precedent: Eltawil et al.\ re-analyse an inflated published result and
recover a realistic figure, and Young et al.'s scoping review finds that
studies at high risk of leakage report higher accuracy than those with
subject-wise splits~\citep{eltawil2026comment,young2025leakage}. We do not claim
to be the first to notice these duplicates. We searched for prior
documentation of them in this benchmark and found none, but absence of
evidence is weak here: a \texttt{drop\_duplicates} result is the kind of
check that is rarely reported. Our contribution here is the measurement: the published band on this dataset is recoverable only when
identical rows are allowed to straddle the split, and after deduplication
macro-recall on this vehicle is 0.580. It is reproducible in either direction
from the committed table. We report deduplicated figures
throughout and state the comparison here, up front, so that our lower
headline number reads as the result of removing duplicates and not as a
weaker model.

\subsection{The model is a vehicle}
\label{sec:data-model}

We compared logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost, each with and
without in-fold SMOTE, under five-fold stratified cross-validation.
Table~\ref{tab:models} gives all six configurations, typeset from
\texttt{results/tables/p1\_model\_metrics.csv}. The primary metric is
macro-recall, with macro-F1, per-class recall, one-versus-rest
ROC-AUC and the confusion matrix as support. Macro-recall is the right
primary here because the costs are asymmetric: failing to flag a high-risk
mother is the error that matters, and headline accuracy on an imbalanced
three-class problem conceals that failure.

The final model is a random forest with no resampling, set to
\texttt{n\_estimators=300} and \texttt{random\_state=42}. It ties the
SMOTE-resampled random forest on macro-recall at 0.580. We broke that tie
deliberately rather than by argmax: the unresampled model has tighter
variance across folds ($\pm 0.027$ versus $\pm 0.040$), the better high-risk
recall (0.723), and no dependence on a synthetic-oversampling step. It also lets SHAP run on the six raw clinical features in their own
units, which is what makes the ASHA tier's driver list expressible as a
template over attributions at all. The selection is a constant in the code, and the
script still prints the argmax winner so the tie stays visible.

We take 0.580 as the operating figure for this deduplicated dataset; the
rendering question exists because models on data like this are
uncertain. Table~\ref{tab:models} says where that uncertainty is concentrated.
Mid-risk recall is $0.227 \pm 0.057$ for the selected model and reaches no
higher than $0.302$ in any of the six configurations, against $0.790$ for low
risk and $0.723$ for high. The mid class is the one the vehicle cannot
resolve, and a rendering that reaches a mother must handle the cases it gets
wrong.

A forest scored on the records it was trained on sends identical feature
vectors to the same leaves, so where those vectors carry two labels, its vote
splits between them and the record looks like a near-tie. Out of fold, each
such record is usually scored by a fold model that saw its twin with the other
label. Of the 71 records on the 35 conflicting vectors
(Section~\ref{sec:data-dedup}), 68 are scored outside the 0.15 margin and 66
are mispredicted; they produce 10 \textsc{red} bands, none for a true
high-risk record (\texttt{results/tables/p1\_gate\_summary.csv}). For this
reason every case in this paper is scored out of fold.

The global attributions come from the final model, fitted to all 451
records: blood sugar is the leading global driver for
every class, with systolic blood pressure second, and diastolic pressure and
heart rate close to noise
(\texttt{results/tables/shap\_global\_mean\_abs.csv}). We write ``blood sugar
and systolic pressure'' throughout because the data supports them as drivers;
``the blood-pressure features'' would include diastolic pressure.

\subsection{Maternal age}
\label{sec:data-age}

The dataset contains very low maternal ages, reflecting adolescent pregnancy
in the source population: 95 of the 451 distinct records are under 18 and the
minimum is 10, both written to \texttt{p1\_cohort\_summary.csv} by the
preparation step. We retain rather than filter these rows, because a deployed
system would see these patients, and dropping them would hide how the model
treats them. The designated failure
case (row 325, Section~\ref{sec:tiers-failure}) is a 17-year-old whose false
high call, out of fold, is driven by her recorded body temperature of
101\,\textdegree F (SHAP $+0.253$), with age contributing almost nothing
($-0.001$). A false high call like this one is why final judgment
stays with a human and why the rendering rules of Section~\ref{sec:tiers}
exist. All data here is public; no real patient or child record is exposed.
